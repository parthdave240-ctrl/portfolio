I built a LinkedIn growth system with Claude. Here's exactly how it works.

A system that researches the right people, qualifies prospects, engages with relevant content, manages connection requests and prepares follow-ups.

I built it around my ICP, positioning, voice and operating rules, using Claude, a cloud-based execution environment and a repository of instructions.

It supports three core workflows: commenting, connection requests and follow-ups. They can run independently or work together as one connected prospecting process.

This page explains how it works, why I built it this way, what went wrong and what I've learned so far.

What it does
Researches LinkedIn profiles, qualifies prospects against an ICP, identifies relevant posts, prepares and publishes comments, manages connection requests with or without personalised notes, and prepares follow-ups after someone accepts. Activities are recorded and reported.
Built with
Claude, a Git repository of growth rules, a cloud-based execution environment, browser workflows and Brevo for prospect and activity logging.
Human in the loop
Research, qualification and message preparation can be handled by the system. Review and approval requirements can be configured for external actions. I started with human approval and continue to review the output.
Built for
Founders managing their own sales, sales teams building warmer prospect relationships, and marketing leaders looking for a consistent LinkedIn prospecting process.
Status
Running since late October 2026. It's still early, so I haven't drawn conclusions about pipeline or revenue impact. I'll add meaningful results as the data builds.

The problem

LinkedIn is an important channel for B2B prospecting, relationship building and sales conversations. But using it consistently takes more work than it appears.

Finding the right people, reading their profiles, understanding what they care about, engaging with their content, sending connection requests and keeping track of conversations takes time.

So prospecting often happens in bursts. People spend a few days finding prospects, get busy with other work and stop.

The alternative is to use automation tools. These can make execution faster, but the quality of the outcome depends on how well the targeting, messaging and workflow are designed.

Poorly configured automation can result in generic comments, irrelevant connection requests and follow-ups that feel disconnected from the original interaction.

There's also a tracking problem. After a few weeks, it becomes difficult to remember who was contacted, what was said, whether a request was accepted and what happened next.

I wanted to build something that brought these activities together.

The goal was simple: the consistency of a system, with the context and judgment of a person.

Three workflows, one LinkedIn growth system

I didn't want to build one large automation that tried to do everything.

Instead, I separated LinkedIn engagement into three workflows. Each has a different purpose, its own instructions and its own operating rules. They can work independently or be connected into a broader prospecting process.

01

Commenting and engagement

Find relevant posts from people who fit the ideal customer profile, understand the context and prepare a short comment in my voice. The objective is to contribute something genuine to a conversation, not to leave generic comments just to increase activity. The system evaluates the post and the author's profile first. If there's nothing meaningful to add, the post can be skipped.

02

Connection requests

Research LinkedIn profiles, identify qualified prospects and prepare connection requests. The workflow checks the prospect's qualification, previous interaction history and applicable rules before preparing the next action.

03

Follow-ups

Track accepted connection requests and prepare contextual follow-up messages, using the existing prospect information and interaction history. It can introduce my portfolio or work when relevant, without immediately turning the conversation into a sales pitch. The idea is to continue a conversation, not start a completely new one.

Connection requests come in two forms:

  • Personalised requests: a short note based on the person's role, work or a relevant interaction.
  • Blank requests: a connection request without a personalised note.

They can run independently or together

These aren't three steps that every prospect must go through.

I can use commenting to engage with relevant people without sending a connection request. I can send a connection request without commenting first. I can also run follow-ups for people who have already accepted a request.

When it makes sense, the workflows can be connected into a single journey:

Research → Qualify → Comment or connect → Acceptance → Follow-up → Activity logging

For example, someone might publish a relevant post. The system identifies them as a suitable prospect, prepares a contextual comment and records the interaction. A connection request can then be considered, followed by a message if they accept.

Alternatively, the system can research a list of profiles, qualify them and prepare connection requests without requiring any commenting activity.

The important part is that the system maintains context across these activities. It knows what has already happened and can use that information to inform the next action. Not every prospect needs to go through every stage.

How it works, day to day

The system isn't restricted to five people or five comments a day. It can research profiles, evaluate prospects against the ICP, identify relevant posts, prepare comments, manage connection requests and prepare follow-ups.

The number of prospects processed and actions performed is configurable. Five people a day is simply the current operating batch I use to keep the process manageable and reviewable.

The workflow

01

Research

Find people through relevant LinkedIn profiles, companies and activity. Research their roles, organisations and professional context.

02

Qualify

Evaluate profiles against the ICP, scoring criteria and exclusion rules. Each prospect receives a score out of ten. Hard exclusion rules come first. If someone fails an important qualification criterion, they're skipped.

03

Identify opportunities

Look for relevant posts, professional achievements or other reasons to start a meaningful interaction. A prospect doesn't need to be active on LinkedIn to qualify. Recent activity simply creates an opportunity for the commenting workflow.

04

Comment

Prepare a short, contextual comment in my voice. The system considers the post and the person rather than relying on generic templates.

05

Connect

Prepare a connection request, with or without a personalised note, depending on the context and configured rules.

06

Follow up

Track accepted requests and prepare contextual follow-up messages based on the existing prospect record.

07

Log and report

Record prospect details, activities, dates, URLs and statuses. Use the activity log to understand what happened and what needs attention.

How much do you hand over?

The level of automation can vary. The important distinction is between preparing an action and executing it.

SetupWhat the system doesWhat you do
Prepare onlyResearches, qualifies, drafts and logsReviews and performs permitted external actions
Approve, then actPrepares actions and proceeds after approvalReviews and approves the relevant actions
Configured executionCarries out permitted configured actions within defined rulesMonitors activity, reviews logs and manages exceptions

I started with approval before action. I recommend starting there too. Moving to a different setup should be a deliberate decision based on the workflow, permissions and applicable platform rules.

How are activity limits decided?

The daily batch size and operating limits are configurable. Five prospects a day is the current routine, not a technical restriction.

The appropriate limits depend on the platform's applicable rules, account permissions, the type of activity, workflow quality and the team's capacity to review and manage interactions.

The objective isn't to maximise activity or disguise automation. It's to build a sustainable, relevant prospecting process that respects LinkedIn's rules. No particular volume or spacing can guarantee that an account won't be restricted.

The structure separates strategy from execution. The ICP defines who to target. The voice file defines how to communicate. Each playbook defines how a particular workflow operates. The tracking layer records what happened.

Why plain files in a Git repository?

  • They're readable by anyone on a team.
  • Every change can be versioned.
  • Rules aren't locked inside a particular automation platform.
  • The AI model can change without requiring the entire growth strategy to be rebuilt.
  • Different workflows can reuse the same underlying audience and brand context.
The tools execute the tasks. The repository holds the decisions behind them.

The ICP: who should the system find?

A system is only as good as the people it points at. I defined the ideal customer profile before building the workflows.

Who

Senior marketing leaders (Head of Marketing, VP of Marketing, CMO, Demand Generation Directors) and founders or co-founders.

Companies

B2B SaaS first, typically seed to Series C or revenue-funded, with approximately 10 to 500 employees. D2C and B2C companies are a secondary audience.

Where

USA and Europe.

Who to skip

Recruiters, agencies selling the same service, students, anyone already contacted, and profiles that fail the qualification rules.

Each prospect receives a score out of ten.

One more rule I added after the first review: activity does not decide eligibility. Someone who rarely posts can still be a great contact. A recent post simply makes the commenting workflow easier to personalise.

Positioning: a careful assistant, not a spam cannon

For founders and sales teams who want LinkedIn to feel like a conversation, this is a configurable growth system that finds relevant people, supports meaningful engagement and keeps a record of what happens.

Five principles guide it:

  • Flexible: research, commenting, connection requests and follow-ups can run independently or together.
  • Personal: each interaction uses relevant profile and content context.
  • Controlled: review and approval requirements can be configured.
  • Recorded: activities are captured in a log that can be reported on.
  • Owned: the underlying rules are maintained in plain files rather than being entirely dependent on one automation platform.

The voice file

Before writing a single message, I wrote down how I sound. I built it from my own writing on my portfolio and LinkedIn profile. It has three traits.

Genuine

Only say what is true and visible. No flattery, no performing enthusiasm.

To the point

Short, plain sentences. One idea at a time.

Giving

Appreciate first. Offer, don't ask. Talk about myself later.

How the rules evolved

My first draft rules produced notes that praised companies and quoted people's About sections. They sounded forced. After reviewing the output, I changed the instructions:

  • There is no single template. Wording changes from person to person.
  • Appreciation must be genuine and proportionate. If it feels stretched, say less.
  • Read everything, refer to little. Avoid unnecessary references to someone's About section or generic company praise.
  • Quiet profiles are still eligible. Write about what they do, or use a blank request when appropriate.

The voice file isn't a library of messages to copy and paste. It's a set of principles that guides how messages are written.

Sounds like me
"Really enjoyed your thinking on partnerships and creators. Would love to connect."

"Congratulations, Maya. Well deserved."
Does not
"Hi, I came across your impressive profile! I am a growth marketer passionate about synergy."

"Great post!"

Examples are fictional.

Comment rules

Comments are one very short line, on relevant marketing or growth posts or professional achievements. No links, nothing about me, no generic filler. If there's nothing genuine to say, the post is skipped.

Guardrails and governance

Giving an AI system access to a LinkedIn workflow requires clear boundaries. These are written into the rulebook and should be reviewed as the system evolves.

01

Account separation

Personal and company work should run in separate browser profiles. The system should use only the account authorised for the relevant activity.

02

Verify before every action

Before an external action, verify that the correct account and execution context are being used. If the expected account cannot be confirmed, stop.

03

Secrets stay secret

API keys and credentials are stored securely. They're never printed, logged, included in screenshots or committed to Git.

04

Configurable activity limits

The current routine uses a small daily batch. The operating limits can be configured based on platform rules, account permissions and the team's capacity. These limits are internal controls, not a guarantee of platform compliance or account safety.

05

Human review

Comments and other external actions follow their configured review requirements. I started with approval enabled. Moving to a different execution setup should be a deliberate decision, with appropriate checks and permissions.

06

Stop on warnings

Any CAPTCHA, verification prompt or warning from LinkedIn stops the run. The system should never attempt to bypass these controls.

07

No unnecessary pitching

The initial interaction should be relevant and genuine. No forced pitch, irrelevant links or unnecessary asks. The portfolio can be introduced later when it makes sense in the conversation.

08

Privacy

Raw profile text and unnecessary personal details should not be stored. Local working files containing prospect information must remain outside public repositories.

09

Respect platform rules

LinkedIn restricts certain forms of unauthorised third-party automation, including automated engagement and connection activity. Human approval, low activity volume and cloud hosting do not remove those restrictions. The implementation must use methods permitted by the platform and respect account permissions and applicable policies. No system can guarantee that an account will never be restricted.

The daily batch and review gate

The batch size is configurable. Five prospects a day is simply the current routine, not a technical restriction.

The system can research and qualify a larger set of profiles, prepare comments, manage connection requests and process follow-ups according to the configured workflows. The batch is a way of managing work, not a limitation of the system's architecture.

Each batch is prepared for review. The review gate prevents work from accumulating faster than it can be checked.

If I haven't reviewed the previous batch, no new batch is created. Once the items have been approved, edited or rejected, the relevant permitted actions can proceed. Rejected prospects are recorded as skipped, so they don't keep coming back.

What went wrong

A build log without mistakes is a brochure. Several of the guardrails above exist because something went wrong.

The first notes were wrong

They praised companies and sounded forced. I rewrote the rules to focus on genuine, specific observations rather than generic compliments.

The free-tier limit

LinkedIn's personalised connection-note allowance affected the original workflow. So the routine evolved to use relevant engagement first, followed by blank connection requests where appropriate. The exact limits can vary by account and platform rules, so this shouldn't be treated as a permanent universal allowance.

A key in a screenshot

An API key appeared in a screenshot. It was revoked and replaced. Keys now live in a protected, ignored file and are never printed.

A typo locked my own folder

A wrong permissions command locked the project folder. It was a one-minute fix, but a useful reminder to keep commands small and verify them before running them.

A missing ignore rule

Local batch files containing people's names could have been committed to GitHub. It was caught before the first batch and is now ignored.

Workflow before automation

One important design principle was to separate the growth process from the tool used to execute it. The ICP, qualification logic, voice and approval requirements need to be defined first. Otherwise, it's easy to build an automation that works technically but doesn't serve the actual sales process.

Questions

Is this safe for my LinkedIn account?

No system can guarantee that an account will never be restricted. LinkedIn restricts certain forms of automated activity, including unauthorised engagement and connection activity. Human review does not remove those restrictions. The system uses defined operating limits, review requirements and stop conditions. These are safeguards, not a guarantee of compliance or account safety. The implementation must follow the platform's current rules and permitted methods.

Is five people a day the limit?

No. Five is the current operating batch size, not a system limitation. The system can research and qualify profiles, identify relevant posts, prepare comments, manage connection requests and prepare follow-ups. The batch size and operating limits are configurable, subject to platform rules, account permissions and workflow requirements.

Do I need LinkedIn Premium?

No. The original implementation was built using a free account. Personalised connection-note allowances can vary by account and platform rules. The workflow supports both personalised and blank requests, so it doesn't depend entirely on one approach.

Can the three workflows run independently?

Yes. Commenting, connection requests and follow-ups are separate workflows. They can run independently or be connected through shared prospect information and interaction history.

Can it send connection requests with or without a note?

Yes. The system supports both personalised and blank connection-request workflows. The choice depends on the context, available functionality, account permissions and configured rules.

Does it automatically comment on posts?

The system can identify relevant posts, evaluate them and prepare comments. The actual publishing process depends on the configured approval requirements and permitted execution method.

Does it follow up after someone accepts?

The system tracks connection status and prepares contextual follow-ups using the existing prospect record. The execution process depends on the configured approval requirements.

Does it need my laptop to be switched on?

The system uses a cloud-based execution environment for its configured workflows, so it doesn't rely on my personal laptop being open for tasks hosted there. The required infrastructure, browser sessions, credentials and connected services still need to be available.

Does it write my LinkedIn posts?

No. This system focuses on researching prospects, engaging with other people's posts and managing prospect interactions. Writing and publishing your own content is a separate workflow.

Does it replace an SDR or a marketer?

No. It supports research, qualification, drafting, tracking and repeatable execution. People are still needed to make decisions, build relationships and handle meaningful sales conversations.

Which tools does it use?

Claude, a Git repository for the rules, a cloud-based execution environment, browser workflows and Brevo for logging and reporting. The exact tools can change depending on the team's existing systems and requirements.

What results can I expect?

It's early, and I'd rather show numbers than promise them. The objective is to make prospecting more consistent, reduce repetitive work and create better visibility into what happens after an interaction. I'll add measured results as the data builds.
