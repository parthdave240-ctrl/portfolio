Why this matters in the AI era

AI has made output cheap. A decent first draft of a message, a sequence or a landing page now takes seconds. What it has not made cheap is knowing who the message should go to, how it should sound, and what must never be said.

That moves the bottleneck from producing to directing. The teams that do well are not the ones with the most output. They are the ones whose output is consistent with a clear strategy.

When AI joins marketing and sales without a shared source of truth, three problems show up:

  • Generic, off-brand output. Every chat starts from nothing, so the result sounds like everyone else's.
  • Drift. Several people and several assistants each hold a different version of the ICP and the tone, and they slowly diverge.
  • Unaccountable action. Once AI can post, send or update records, "who approved this, and why was it allowed?" becomes a real question.
AI amplifies whatever you give it. A growth repo makes sure that is a clear strategy, not a guess.

A growth repo answers all three. It is the briefing the assistant reads, the rulebook it follows, and the record of what happened.

What it is designed to give you

On-ICP targeting

One definition of a good lead, used in every channel and tool.

On-voice messages

Drafts that sound like you, because the voice is written down with examples.

Safe automation

Approval, caps, account checks and stop rules before AI acts for you.

A complete record

Every action logged, and every rule change in version history.

A learning loop

A weekly report that turns results into rule changes.

Easy handover

A new hire, contractor or agency starts from the same rules.

What a growth repo is

Most growth teams have the same assets scattered in the same places: the ICP in a slide deck, the tone of voice in someone's head, the outreach rules in a Notion page nobody updates, and the real history buried in four different tools.

A growth repo pulls the rules into one place as plain files. It does not replace your tools. Your CRM still stores contacts, your email tool still sends sequences, LinkedIn is still where conversations happen. The repo is the layer above them that says who, how, when, and what is not allowed.

Tools hold the data. The repo holds the decisions.

That split matters because decisions are what teams lose: why this audience, why this tone, why this cap. When they live in files with version history, they can be read, argued with, improved and handed over.

ICP, positioning, differentiation, voice

These four files are the core. Write them before any outreach, because every message depends on them.

The ICP

Who you target and how you decide, written as a rule and not a feeling. In my repo it names two groups (senior marketing leaders, and founders), company fit in tiers (B2B SaaS first, D2C and B2C second), regions, and a score out of ten with a decision rule: 7 or more connect, 5 to 6 comes to me, 4 or less skip. It also lists who to always skip, such as agencies selling the same service.

Positioning

One clear sentence on what you are for and who, plus the reasons it is true. Positioning in the repo is not a slogan. It is the reference every message is checked against: if a line does not fit the positioning, it does not go out.

Differentiation

What makes you different from the three alternatives a buyer actually considers: doing nothing, doing it themselves, or hiring someone else. Written as a short table, it keeps messaging honest and stops you claiming things you cannot show.

Voice

How you sound, with examples of good and bad. Mine has three traits (genuine, to the point, giving) and rules such as "appreciate first, talk about yourself later". It is built from real writing, not adjectives.

Why files and not a deck: the AI assistant reads them directly, so the same ICP and voice shape every draft, and you can change a rule once and see it applied everywhere.

LinkedIn outreach playbooks

Built and running

This is the part I have built and run. It is split by activity, because each has different rules.

01

Daily run

Five people a day. Find a relevant post, draft a short comment, review it, post it, then send a request. Counted from the log, not from memory.

02

Commenting

Which posts to pick (marketing content or achievements), and how to write a comment: short, plain, genuine, nothing about you.

03

Connection requests

Hard gates before any request, the scoring rule, how to write a note, cooldowns, and the follow-up after someone accepts.

04

Batch preparation and the review gate

A scheduled task prepares the next batch without posting anything. No new batch is created until the last one is reviewed.

I wrote the full story of this system on its own page: how I built the LinkedIn growth system.

Email outreach playbooks

The pattern, not live in my repo

I have not built or run email outreach in my own repo yet, so I will not claim results. This is how the same structure would carry it, and it is the layout I would set up for a team that wants it.

  • Who gets emailed. The same ICP and scoring as LinkedIn. One definition of a good lead, used everywhere.
  • One channel at a time per person. A rule that stops the same prospect getting a LinkedIn touch and a sequence on the same day.
  • Copy rules. Length, tone and what is banned, taken from the voice file. Sequences are written as files, reviewed, and versioned.
  • Deliverability rules. Sending limits, warm-up, verified addresses only, and when to stop. These are the things that quietly damage a domain if nobody owns them.
  • Reply handling. Which replies need a human, which stop the sequence, and how outcomes are logged.
  • An approval step. The same review gate: nothing is added to a live sequence until a person approves the copy and the list.

Three ways a tool connects

  • Through a script. A small, reviewed script writes to the tool's API (this is how my Brevo log works). The script is in the repo, so you can read exactly what it does.
  • Through the browser. The assistant operates a tool's own interface under your account, with verification and approval. This is how LinkedIn is handled.
  • Through exports. Where there is no safe API access, exports are reviewed by a person and kept out of the repo.

The same split applies to any CRM: the repo says what a field means and who may write it, and the CRM holds the actual records. Personal data stays in the tools, never in the repo.

Guardrails and governance

When an AI assistant can act in your accounts, the rules about what it may do are as important as the rules about what it should write.

Account separation

Personal and company accounts stay apart. The assistant verifies which account is signed in before any action.

Human approval

Posts, sends and list changes wait for a person. Automation is switched on later, deliberately.

Caps and cooldowns

Daily limits and wait times between touches, counted from the log.

Secrets stay out

Keys live in one ignored file and are never printed or committed.

Stop conditions

Any warning, verification prompt or unexpected account stops the run.

No personal data in Git

Contact lists, exports and drafts with names stay local or in the CRM.

Tracking and reporting

If the work is not logged, it cannot be improved. The repo defines the log: what fields exist, what they mean, and when each one is written. A small script produces a weekly report from the log.

How to set one up

01

Create the repo

A private Git repository. Add a short README (the map) and a CLAUDE.md (the rules the assistant follows).

02

Set the ground rules first

An ignore file for secrets and local data, a note on which accounts may be used, and the rule that active work lives in only a few folders.

03

Write the ICP

Groups, company fit, regions, a score and a decision rule, and who to always skip.

04

Write positioning, differentiation and voice

From real material: your site, calls, past emails. Include good and bad examples.

05

Choose the log

Pick the CRM that will hold the record, and define the fields before you write any data.

06

Write the first playbook

Start with one channel. Write the daily routine, the gates, the caps and the stop rules.

07

Dry run

Run it on real profiles or leads with nothing sent. Edit the rules from what you see.

08

Approval phase

Review everything before it goes out. Track edits, and use them to improve the rules.

09

Add the report

A weekly report from the log. Read it every week and change one thing at a time.

10

Add the next channel

Email, then lifecycle or events. Each gets its own playbook folder and reuses the same ICP and voice.

Mistakes to avoid

Copying a template

My first outreach rules produced forced, template-sounding notes. Rules written from your own voice beat a borrowed template.

Ignoring platform limits

LinkedIn's free tier allowed only five personalised notes a month. Check limits early and design around them.

Secrets in screenshots

An API key appeared in a screenshot and had to be replaced. Keep keys in one ignored file and never show them on screen.

A missing ignore rule

Local files with people's names almost went to GitHub. Set the ignore rules before you create any data.

Trusting a default

The default browser was signed in as someone else's account. Always verify the account before acting, never assume.

Padding the quota

Some days there are fewer than five good targets. Doing fewer beats filling the quota with weak matches.

Setting one up for your team

If you want a growth repo set up for your team, I would start with a working session on your ICP and voice, then build the structure around the channel you care about most. The phases and the review gate are the same as for any team: define, build, dry run, approve, then run daily. You stay in control of your accounts, your voice and your data.

Questions

Do I need to be technical?

No. The repo is text files, and the assistant does the technical parts. You need to be able to read and edit documents, and to review what it proposes.

Does this replace my CRM or email tool?

No. Your tools keep their jobs. The repo holds the rules and the definitions they should follow.

Is it only for LinkedIn?

No. LinkedIn is the channel I built first. The structure carries email, lifecycle and events the same way, each with its own playbook folder.

Have you connected Lemlist and HubSpot?

Not in my own repo. Brevo and the browser are live. Lemlist and HubSpot are shown as the pattern for how the repo would connect, and I would build and test that with you before relying on it.

What results can I expect?

It is early, and I would rather show numbers than promise them. The reliable gains are consistency, a record of everything, and review before anything goes out. Conversion results need a few months of data.

Is it safe for my accounts?

Platforms limit automated activity and no tool can guarantee an account is never restricted. A growth repo keeps volume low, requires approval, verifies the account and stops on warnings, which lowers the risk but does not remove it.
