I built a LinkedIn growth system with Claude. Here is exactly how it works.

A small system that finds the right people, engages with them like a person, logs everything, and keeps a human in charge. I built it from an empty folder, and this page explains every part, including what went wrong.

What it does
Finds relevant posts by people who fit my ideal customer profile, drafts a short comment for each, and after I approve, comments and sends a connection request. Every step is logged and reported weekly.
Built with
Claude (reads profiles, drafts, operates the browser), Chrome, Brevo as the log, and a Git repo of plain-text rules.
Human in the loop
I approve, edit or reject every comment before it is posted. Nothing goes out on its own.
Built for
Founders doing their own sales, sales teams that want warmer outreach, and marketing leaders who want a consistent LinkedIn presence.
Status
Running since late October 2026. It is early, so I have not drawn conclusions from results yet. I will add them to this page as they come in.

The problem

LinkedIn is where most B2B buying conversations start, and most outreach there fails for one of two reasons.

It is manual. Finding the right people, reading their profiles, writing something real, and logging it all takes hours. So it happens in bursts and stops when the week gets busy.

Or it is automated badly. Templates, high volume, and no one checking what goes out. It reads like automation, the replies show it, and the account takes the risk.

What I wanted sits in between: the consistency of a system, with the judgment and tone of a person.

There is a third problem that hides behind both: nobody can say afterwards what was sent, to whom, or what happened next. A system worth having keeps its own record.

How it works, day to day

Five people a day. That is deliberate: small enough to stay personal, large enough to build a pipeline over weeks.

01

Find

Recent posts by people who fit the ICP, in the right regions. Marketing or growth content, or an achievement worth congratulating.

02

Read and score

Claude reads the post and the author's profile (read-only), then scores the person out of ten against the ICP. Hard gates come first: if they fail, the person is skipped.

03

Draft

One short comment in my voice. Genuine, specific, nothing about me, no links.

04

Review

I see the post, the person and the draft. I approve, edit, or reject each one.

05

Engage

Approved comments are posted. Later, after looking at the profile again, a blank connection request goes out, spaced through the day.

06

Log

The comment, the post, the dates, and the request are recorded in a CRM-style log. Nothing is kept in anyone's head.

07

Follow up

When someone accepts, a short, warm message goes out with my portfolio as something that might be useful. No pitch, no ask.

The foundation: a repo of rules

The first thing I built was not a script. It was a folder of plain-text files that explain the whole system in words: who to target, how to sound, what is off limits, and what the daily routine is. Claude reads these at the start of every session. A file called CLAUDE.md is the rulebook.

├── tracking/linkedin/        log schema, weekly report

└── projects/ templates/ skills/ campaigns/ experiments/ archive/

Why plain files in a Git repo? They are readable by anyone on a team, every change is versioned, and nothing is locked inside a tool. If the AI changes, the rules stay.

The ICP

A system is only as good as who it points at. I wrote the ideal customer profile before anything else.

Who

Senior marketing leaders (Head of Marketing, VP, CMO, demand gen directors) and founders or co-founders.

Companies

B2B SaaS first, seed to Series C or revenue-funded, roughly 10 to 500 people. D2C and B2C second.

Where

USA and Europe.

Skip

Recruiters, agencies selling the same service, students, anyone already contacted.

The score

Each profile gets a score out of ten. The weights are easy to change per client.

CriterionPoints
Title fits a target group0 to 3
Company stage and size match0 to 2
Funding or revenue signal0 to 2
Company type and growth need match my experience0 to 2 (B2B SaaS up to 2, D2C or B2C up to 1)
Reachable and active on LinkedIn0 to 1

7 or more: connect. 5 to 6: comes to me to decide. 4 or less: skip. Because D2C can earn at most one point on company type, it needs a near-perfect score elsewhere to qualify. That is how "secondary" is enforced in numbers rather than intentions.

One more rule I added after the first review: activity does not decide eligibility. Someone who rarely posts can still be a great contact. A recent post just makes the comment easier to personalise.

Positioning

A careful assistant, not a spam cannon.

For founders and sales teams who want LinkedIn to feel like a conversation, this is a small AI-run workflow that finds the right people, engages like a person, and keeps a human in charge.

  • Small on purpose. Five people a day, not five hundred.
  • Personal. Every comment is written for one post and one person.
  • Reviewed. A human approves before anything is posted.
  • Recorded. Every action lands in a log you can report on.
  • Owned. The rules are plain files you keep, not settings inside someone else's tool.

The voice file

Before writing a single message I wrote down how I sound. I built it from my own writing on my portfolio and profile. It has three traits.

Genuine

Only say what is true and visible. No flattery, no performing enthusiasm.

To the point

Short, plain sentences. One idea each.

Giving

Appreciate first. Offer, do not ask. Talk about myself later.

How the rules evolved

My first draft rules produced notes that praised companies and quoted people's About sections. They read as forced. After review I rewrote them:

  • There is no single template. The wording changes from note to note.
  • Appreciation must be genuine and proportionate. If it would feel stretched, say less.
  • Read everything, refer to little. No direct references to someone's About section, no company praise as the reason.
  • Quiet profiles are still eligible. Write about what they do, or send a blank request.
Sounds like me
"Really enjoyed your thinking on partnerships and creators. Would love to connect."

"Congratulations, Maya. Well deserved."
Does not
"Hi, I came across your impressive profile! I am a growth marketer passionate about synergy."

"Great post!"

Examples are fictional.

Comment rules

Comments are one very short line, on marketing or growth posts or on achievements. No links, nothing about me, no generic filler. If there is nothing genuine to say, the post is skipped.

Guardrails

Giving an AI access to a LinkedIn account needs boundaries. These are written into the rulebook and checked every time.

01

Account separation

Personal and company work run in separate browser profiles. The AI only uses the personal profile for personal outreach.

02

Verify before every action

Before anything on LinkedIn, it confirms the signed-in account is mine. If not, it stops.

03

Secrets stay secret

API keys live in one ignored file. They are never printed, logged, or committed.

04

A daily cap

Five people a day, counted from the log, not from memory.

05

Human approval

Comments are approved before they are posted. Automation is something I can switch on later, not a starting point.

06

Stop on warnings

Any CAPTCHA, verification prompt, or warning from LinkedIn stops the run. It never tries to get around one.

07

Nothing about me, nothing pushy

The first touch contains no pitch, no link, and no ask. The portfolio only appears after someone accepts.

08

Privacy

Raw profile text and personal details are not stored. Local batch files stay off GitHub.

The log and the report

Brevo is the system of record. Every person becomes a contact keyed by their LinkedIn handle, with no email needed to start. They are not blocked from email either, so I can segment and email them later if it makes sense.

GroupFields
WhoName, profile URL, job title, company, location
FitCompany type, target group, ICP score
EngagementType (comment, request with note, blank request, comment then request), status
CommentThe exact comment, the date, the post URL
RequestThe note (or blank), the day it was sent, the day it was accepted
Follow-upThe message and when it was sent

A weekly report reads this log and shows, week on week: comments, requests sent, accepted, still pending, and the acceptance rate. It also compares requests with a note against blank ones, so the numbers can settle arguments that opinions cannot.

The daily batch and review gate

Each morning a scheduled task is set to prepare the day's batch: five posts, five authors, five draft comments. This preparation is read-only. It never comments, never connects, never reacts.

Then comes the rule I like best: if I have not reviewed yesterday's batch, no new batch is created. It cannot pile up and it cannot run ahead of me. When I have approved, edited, or rejected every item, the approved ones are posted and the next batch can be prepared. Rejected people are logged as skipped, so they never come back.

What went wrong

A build log without mistakes is a brochure. Several of the guardrails above exist because something went wrong.

The first notes were wrong

They praised companies and sounded forced. I rewrote the rules.

The free-tier limit

LinkedIn allowed me only five personalised notes a month. So the routine became: comment first, then send a blank request.

A key in a screenshot

An API key appeared in a screenshot. It was revoked and replaced. Keys now live in one ignored file and are never printed.

A typo locked my own folder

A wrong permissions command locked the project folder. A one-minute fix, and a reminder to keep commands small.

A missing ignore rule

Local batch files with people's names would have been committed to GitHub. It was caught before the first batch and is now ignored.

Who it is useful for

Founders

Early sales are founder-led, and LinkedIn is usually where the first conversations happen. The system replaces hours of finding people and writing messages with a short daily review, while the founder's voice stays intact.

Sales teams

Warm up accounts before outbound lands. Keep a consistent presence in the posts your buyers read. Give sales leaders a record of what was done and a weekly view of what is working, instead of relying on each rep's habits.

Marketing leaders

Support social selling and account-based plays with steady, human engagement, and measure it.

Deploying it for your team

If you want a system like this set up for you or your team, this is how I would approach it.

What I set up

  • Your ICP and scoring, written down and agreed.
  • Your voice file, built from your own writing.
  • The rulebook, guardrails, and daily routine.
  • A log in your CRM, with the fields and a weekly report.
  • A daily batch with a review gate, so nothing happens without you.

What I need from you

  • A short working session on who you sell to, and samples of how you write.
  • Your own LinkedIn account, signed in on your computer. I do not need your password.
  • A CRM or Brevo account for the log.
  • A few minutes a day to review the batch.

The phases

  1. Define. ICP, voice, and rules.
  2. Build. The repo, the log, and the guardrails.
  3. Dry run. Real profiles, nothing sent, until you trust the drafts.
  4. Approval phase. You review every comment before it is posted.
  5. Daily batch. The batch is prepared each morning, and you decide how much to automate over time.
You stay in control throughout: your account, your voice, your approval, your data.

Questions

Is this safe for my LinkedIn account?

LinkedIn limits automated activity, and no tool can promise an account will never be restricted. This system is deliberately small, spaced out, and human-approved, and it stops on any warning. That lowers the risk, but it does not remove it.

Do I need LinkedIn Premium?

No. I built this on a free account. The free tier limits personalised connection notes to a few a month, so the routine uses comments first and blank requests after.

Does it write my posts?

No. It engages with other people's posts. Writing your own content is a separate job, and one I would do with you, not automatically.

Does it replace an SDR or a marketer?

No. It handles finding, reading, drafting and logging, so a person can spend their time on the conversations that follow.

Which tools does it use?

Claude, a Chrome browser, a CRM or Brevo for the log, and a Git repo for the rules. Nothing proprietary.
